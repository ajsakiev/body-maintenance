# Kettlebell AMRAP Program

A strength-and-conditioning regimen built around the **20-minute AMRAP** — *as many rounds as possible* of a short circuit, against a fixed clock. One or two kettlebells, a few square metres of floor, three sessions a week.

The format deserves a program built around it. It is time-capped, so a session never sprawls. It is self-scoring, so progress is visible without any calculation. And a kettlebell loads the hinge, squat, press, pull, and carry more completely than almost any other single tool. But a stack of unrelated 20-minute burners is not a program — it's the same medium-hard effort repeated until it stops working. What follows adds the pieces that make the format compound: a fixed benchmark, three distinct session intents, a load ladder, and rules for when to push and when to hold.

It stands alone, or sits alongside the [Year One Gym Program](content/exercise/strength/gym-program.md) — see *Combining With the Gym* below.

## What a 20-Minute AMRAP Actually Trains

Be clear-eyed about what the format does well and where it has a ceiling. This is what the three-intent week below is designed around.

| Quality | What the format does for it | Ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| **Work capacity / muscular endurance** | The primary adaptation. Sustaining sub-maximal output under accumulating fatigue is exactly what AMRAP trains | None practical |
| **Aerobic fitness** | Continuous kettlebell circuits hold heart rate in the vigorous range — typically 75–90% of max — for the full 20 minutes | Real, but improves VO₂ max less than dedicated intervals |
| **Maximal strength** | Meaningful for a returning lifter; the swing alone raises max and explosive strength | Plateaus once the bell stops being heavy relative to you |
| **Power** | The swing is a ballistic hinge — one of the few power movements safe to repeat for hundreds of reps | Fatigue erodes it late in a session |
| **Grip and posterior chain** | Constant. Every swing, row, and carry loads the hands and the back of the body | None practical |
| **Body composition** | High energy cost per minute; preserves muscle while lean | Diet still decides the outcome |

> [Source: Farrar, Mayhew & Koch (2010) — oxygen cost of continuous kettlebell swings](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Farrar+Mayhew+Koch+oxygen+cost+kettlebell+swings) [MODERATE]

> [Source: Lake & Lauder (2012) — kettlebell swing training improves maximal and explosive strength](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Lake+Lauder+kettlebell+swing+training+maximal+explosive+strength) [MODERATE]

> [Source: Manocchia et al. (2013) — transference of kettlebell training to strength, power, and endurance](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Manocchia+kettlebell+training+transference+strength+power+endurance) [MODERATE]

Kettlebell programs have also reduced neck, shoulder, and low-back pain in working adults over eight weeks — the posterior-chain loading is doing something useful beyond fitness. [MODERATE]

> [Source: Jay et al. (2011) — kettlebell training for musculoskeletal and cardiovascular health, randomised controlled trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Jay+kettlebell+training+musculoskeletal+cardiovascular+health+randomized)

**The honest limitation:** left to itself, every AMRAP drifts to the same intensity — too heavy to be easy aerobic work, too light and too fatigued to be real strength work. The fix is not to abandon the format. It is to give each session in the week a different job.

## The Design in One Table

| Variable | Setting | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 3 sessions/week (2 minimum, 4 maximum) | Enough to progress; recoverable alongside a life |
| Session length | 20-min AMRAP + 8-min warm-up + 5-min cool-down ≈ 35 min | The AMRAP is the work; the bookends are what keep you doing it in a year |
| Movements per round | 3–4 | Long enough for variety, short enough to remember and count |
| Round length | 45–90 seconds | Keeps the count honest and the pacing readable |
| Reps per movement | 5–10 (3–5 on the heavy day) | Low enough that rep 8 of round 15 looks like rep 1 of round 1 |
| Load | A bell you could do **double** the prescribed reps with, fresh | Leaves the margin that fatigue will consume |
| Form standard | The round ends when form breaks, not when the reps are done | Ballistic lifts under fatigue are where the injuries live |
| Calories | Maintenance | See [Relative Strength](content/exercise/strength/relative-strength.md) — training decides strength, diet decides size |

## Three Session Intents

Same format, three different jobs. This is polarised training applied inside a single tool: most of the week's work is genuinely easy or genuinely heavy, and only one session lives in the hard middle. The same distribution governs how endurance athletes train, and it works for the same reasons. [STRONG]

> [Source: Seiler (2010) — best practice for training intensity distribution](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Seiler+2010+training+intensity+distribution+endurance+athletes)

| Day | Intent | Effort | Bell | Reps | Breathing | Success looks like |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Pace** | Aerobic base, movement practice | 6–7/10 | Working bell | 8–10 | Nose only, could speak in short sentences | Even splits, same rounds in the second 10 min as the first |
| **Heavy** | Strength | 8/10 per rep, fully rested between | One size up | 3–5 | Rest until you could hold a conversation | 8–12 rounds with zero missed or ugly reps |
| **Push** | Performance | 9/10 | Working bell | 5–8 | Mouth breathing fine in the last 5 min | Beat the log |

The Pace day is where most people go wrong. It will feel too easy. That is the point — it is doing the same job as [Zone 2](content/exercise/cardio/zone2-training.md) work, and it is the day that makes the Push day possible. If your nose breathing fails, you have drifted into a Push session, and you now have two of them in a week and no base.

## The Movement Pool

Everything you need, organised by pattern. Each column to the right is harder than the one before. Do not skip columns.

| Pattern | Foundation | Progression | Advanced | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hinge — ballistic** | Two-hand swing | One-arm swing | Snatch | The engine of the program. Every round has one |
| **Hinge — grind** | Kettlebell deadlift | Single-leg deadlift | Double-bell deadlift | Heavy-day staple |
| **Squat** | Goblet squat | Front squat, rack position | Double front squat | Goblet stays useful forever |
| **Lunge** | Goblet reverse lunge | Rack reverse lunge | Double rack lunge | Reverse, not forward — kinder to the knee |
| **Vertical push** | Strict press | Push press | Double press | Ribs down, lock out, bell over the shoulder joint |
| **Horizontal push** | Push-up | Push-up on the bell handle | Deficit or weighted push-up | Bodyweight is fine; keep it in |
| **Pull** | Bent-over row | Renegade row | Double row | The one pattern kettlebells under-serve — never drop it |
| **Carry / core** | Suitcase carry | Rack carry | Overhead carry | Distances, not reps |
| **Shoulder health** | Halo | Windmill | Turkish get-up | Warm-up and cool-down, not inside the clock |

**Every round contains a hinge**, and at least one of push, pull, or squat. Across a week, all six patterns should appear.

**Not inside an AMRAP:** the Turkish get-up (too slow, quality-first — it belongs in the warm-up), snatches until your one-arm swing is clean and boring, and anything you learned this week.

> [Source: Otto et al. (2012) — weightlifting vs. kettlebell training: strength, power and body composition](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Otto+weightlifting+kettlebell+training+vertical+jump+strength+body+composition) [MODERATE]

## The Three Sessions

These are the Block 1 templates. Later blocks swap movements one column to the right along the pool above; the structure never changes.

### Session 1 — Pace *(the Benchmark)*

20-minute AMRAP. Nose breathing throughout.

| # | Movement | Reps |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Two-hand swing | 10 |
| 2 | Goblet squat | 5 |
| 3 | Push-up | 5 |
| 4 | Bent-over row | 5 per side |

Target: 14–18 rounds on first exposure, at a bell you can swing 20 clean reps with when fresh. Twenty rounds is 200 swings — that is a lot of hinge, and it is the trigger to move up a bell (below).

### Session 2 — Heavy

20-minute AMRAP, but the clock is a cap, not a target. Rest as long as you need between rounds. If you get more than 12 rounds, the bell is too light.

| # | Movement | Reps |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Clean & strict press | 3 per side |
| 2 | Front squat, rack position | 5 per side |
| 3 | Kettlebell deadlift *(heaviest bell you have)* | 5 |
| 4 | Suitcase carry | 20 m per side |

Target: 8–12 rounds, every rep clean. This day is closest in spirit to the [Year One Gym Program](content/exercise/strength/gym-program.md) — heavy enough to drive neural adaptation, rested enough that the load stays honest.

### Session 3 — Push

20-minute AMRAP. Hard, but the form standard still ends the set.

| # | Movement | Reps |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | One-arm swing *(or 16 two-hand)* | 8 per side |
| 2 | Goblet reverse lunge | 5 per side |
| 3 | Push press | 5 per side |
| 4 | Halo | 5 per direction |

Target: whatever beats the last Push session by a round or a partial. Log it.

**Counting "per side":** one round means both sides done. When you note partial rounds, note the movement and rep you reached — "12 rounds + 10 swings + 3 squats".

**Scheduling:** never back to back if you can avoid it. Mon / Wed / Fri or Tue / Thu / Sat, Pace first in the week, Push last, so the hardest day is followed by the longest gap.

## The Benchmark

Session 1 is your benchmark. Run it in weeks 1, 4, 8, and 12, and hold everything constant: same bell, same standards, same warm-up, same rough time of day. Rounds plus partial reps is your score.

This single number is what tells you whether the program is working. Individual Push sessions bounce around with sleep and stress; the benchmark every four weeks does not.

Realistic expectations for a returning lifter: **20–35% more rounds over the first twelve weeks**, most of it in weeks 1–8. When you reach 20 rounds at a given bell, the benchmark moves up one bell size and your score will drop by roughly a quarter. That drop is progress, not regression — you are now doing the same work with more load.

## The Twelve-Week Arc

Three blocks and a reset. The exercises change one column per block; the templates and the three intents never do.

| Block | Weeks | The week | Effort caps | What changes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Foundation** | 1–4 | Pace · Pace · Push *(capped at 8/10)* | Nose breathing on all Pace days | Learn the movements, establish the benchmark, build the habit |
| **2. Build** | 5–8 | Pace · Heavy · Push | Push at full 9/10; Heavy day introduced | Move up a bell on Heavy day when you reach 12 clean rounds |
| **3. Push** | 9–11 | Pace · Heavy · Push *(+ optional 4th Pace)* | None — chase the log | Progression column moves one step right on any movement that feels routine |
| **4. Reset** | 12 | Short Pace · rest · **Benchmark retest** | Two 10-minute AMRAPs at 6/10, then the test | Log the score, then restart the arc at a higher floor |

### Block 1 — Foundation (Weeks 1–4)

The block that decides whether the swing becomes an asset or a back complaint. Two Pace days a week means a lot of low-stress reps to groove the hinge before you ever push the pace.

- Run the Benchmark in week 1 at an effort you could repeat tomorrow. Record it
- The Push day is capped at 8/10 — hard, but with a round left in the tank
- Film a set of swings from the side in week 2. You are looking for a flat back, hips going *back* rather than down, and the bell floating to chest height on hip snap alone, arms relaxed
- Expect some hand soreness and posterior-chain DOMS in weeks 1–2, then a sharp drop-off. See [DOMS & Soreness](content/exercise/recovery/doms-soreness.md)
- Retest the Benchmark in week 4

### Block 2 — Build (Weeks 5–8)

- The Heavy day arrives. Move to a bell one size up (4 kg / ~9 lb) for it. Presses may need to become push presses at first; that is fine
- Push day at full effort, but form still ends the set
- Pace day stays nasal. If it is getting easy, add one round rather than speed — or move the swings to one-arm and keep the count
- Retest the Benchmark in week 8

### Block 3 — Push (Weeks 9–11)

- Any movement that feels routine moves one column right: two-hand swing → one-arm; goblet squat → rack front squat; strict press → push press; push-up → push-up on the bell
- **One variable per movement per block.** Do not move up a bell *and* change the variant in the same week
- An optional fourth session is allowed only if it is a Pace day. Never a second Push
- Rounds falling across two consecutive Push days with normal sleep means you are carrying fatigue — take the deload a week early

### Week 12 — Reset and Retest

- Two easy 10-minute AMRAPs at 6/10 early in the week
- Retest the Benchmark at the end of the week, fresh
- Compare week 12 to week 1. Then restart Block 1 with one change: a heavier bell, or the next movement column — not both

## Progression Rules

Four rules, in priority order. When two of them disagree, the one higher up wins.

**1. Rounds first.** At a fixed bell and fixed reps, more rounds in 20 minutes is more work in the same time — density progression. It is the primary lever and it is fully honest, because you cannot fake a round. Aim to beat the last comparable session by one round or a partial. If you beat it by three, you were sandbagging last time; note that too.

**2. Load second.** Two triggers move you up a bell size:

- Benchmark (Pace) reaches **20 rounds**
- Heavy day reaches **12 rounds with every rep clean**

Standard bells step in 4 kg increments (8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32 kg — roughly 18, 26, 35, 44, 53, 62, 70 lb). One step is a 17–25% load jump, so expect rounds to fall by about that and take 3–4 weeks to climb back. That climb is where the strength is built.

**3. Complexity last.** Change a movement variant only at a block boundary, and only when the current one is boring. Novelty feels like progress and mostly isn't.

**4. Form ends the set.** Each movement has a standard. The first rep that misses it ends that movement for the round — rest 20–30 seconds, then continue the round. Missing standards twice in a session means today's bell is too heavy or the reps too high; adjust down and finish. Never chase the count through broken reps.

| Movement | Standard |
|---|---|
| Swing | Flat back at the bottom, hips snap to lockout, bell floats to chest height with relaxed arms, no squatting |
| Goblet / front squat | Below parallel, heels down, elbows inside the knees, torso upright |
| Press | Full lockout with the bicep by the ear, ribs down, no lean-back |
| Push-up | Chest to fist height, body in one line, elbows at ~45° |
| Row | Torso still, bell to the hip, shoulder blade pulling the weight, not the arm |
| Lunge | Rear knee kisses the floor, front shin near vertical, no wobble on the way up |
| Carry | Tall, ribs down, shoulders level, no lean away from the bell |

## Pacing a 20-Minute AMRAP

The skill that separates people who progress on this format from people who just get tired.

- **Even splits.** Note your round count at 10 minutes. The second half should match it. Almost everyone starts too fast; the first five minutes should feel like a warm-up
- **Rest before the swing, not after it.** The swing is the most demanding movement in the round. Take your breaths standing over the bell, then hit it fresh
- **Bell-down rest.** When you rest, put the bell down. Holding it in the rack or hanging from the handle is grip work you are not counting
- **Grip is the usual limiter.** Chalk helps. Alternate hands on one-arm work every set. When grip fails on one-arm swings, switch to two-hand and keep moving rather than stopping
- **The last three minutes are where the rounds are.** Do not spend them in the first three
- **Breath tells you the day.** Nose breathing means Pace. Mouth breathing means Push. Gasping means the round should have ended already

## Choosing Bells

You need at most two. Pick them by test, not by chart.

| Bell | Test | What it's for |
|---|---|---|
| **Working bell** | 20 clean two-hand swings and 8–10 strict presses per side, fresh, with reps in reserve | Pace and Push days, all movements |
| **Heavy bell** | One size up from the working bell. 10 swings, 3–5 presses per side | Heavy day, deadlifts, suitcase carries |

Common starting points — adjust to your own tests: 12–16 kg working bell for most adults new to kettlebells, 16–24 kg for someone with a lifting background. The working bell will move up over the year; the old working bell becomes your warm-up and press bell.

**If you have only one bell,** adjust the movement, not the bell: push press instead of strict press when it is too heavy to press, and one-arm swings or longer carries when it is too light for the hinge. The single-bell version of this program works; it just runs out of road sooner on the Heavy day.

## Session Structure

**Warm-up — 8 minutes, every session:**

1. 2 min easy movement — brisk walk, skipping, jog in place. Raise temperature, nothing more
2. 3–4 movements from the [Daily Mobility Routine](content/exercise/mobility/daily-routine.md), biased to [hips](content/exercise/mobility/hips.md) and [thoracic spine](content/exercise/mobility/thoracic-spine.md)
3. Bell ramp with the working bell: 10 halos, 5 goblet squats with a 3-second pause at the bottom, 2 × 10 swings building to full speed, 3 presses per side
4. **Heavy day only:** one slow Turkish get-up per side before the ramp

The ramp is not optional. The first ten swings tell you what today's hinge looks like before you commit to twenty minutes of it.

**During:** a visible timer, a way to count rounds you don't have to think about (a tally on paper, or a clicker), and water within reach.

**After — 5 minutes:** walk until your heart rate is under 110. Then a couch stretch or kneeling hip-flexor stretch per side, and a dead hang from a bar if you have one. The hip flexors and forearms are what this program tightens.

## Combining With the Gym

Both programs pull toward the same outcome. The question is only which one leads.

| Setup | The week | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Kettlebell standalone** | Pace · Heavy · Push | For stretches without gym access, travel, or when you simply prefer it. Fully sufficient |
| **Gym leads** | Gym A · KB Pace · Gym B · KB Push | Drop the KB Heavy day — the gym covers heavy. Kettlebells become conditioning on the off days |
| **Kettlebell leads** | KB Pace · Gym A *(main lifts only, 30 min)* · KB Push | Keep one gym session for the heavy squat and hinge that a bell can't load. Drop the accessories; the AMRAPs cover them |

Concurrent strength and conditioning training does interfere — but mostly with power, mostly when the endurance volume is high, and mostly when both are done in the same session. Kettlebell AMRAPs are low-volume and strength-flavoured; keep them on separate days from heavy lifting and the interference is small. If they must share a day, lift first and leave at least six hours. [MODERATE]

> [Source: Wilson et al. (2012) — concurrent training meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Wilson+2012+concurrent+training+meta-analysis+strength+endurance)

**Watch the hinge total.** Swings, deadlifts, RDLs, and kettlebell deadlifts all load the same tissue. Four sessions a week that each hinge heavily is how low backs get cranky. In a hybrid week, the gym hinge day and the KB Heavy day should not both exist.

## What to Track

One line per session, in any notes app.

```
Date | Session (Pace / Heavy / Push) | Bell | Rounds + partial | RPE /10 | Nasal? Y/N | Notes
```

The Benchmark score every four weeks answers the big question. The Push log answers the weekly one — *did I beat last time?* The Nasal column keeps the Pace day honest, which is the column people stop filling in first, and it's the one that matters most.

## Missed Time

| Time off | What to do |
|---|---|
| Up to 1 week | Nothing. Resume the week you were in |
| 2–3 weeks | Two Pace sessions at 6/10, then resume |
| 4–8 weeks | Drop one bell size for two weeks, then climb back |
| 2+ months | Restart Block 1 with your last working bell. You'll be back in three weeks |

Conditioning fades faster than strength — you'll feel the first session back more than a gym lifter would. The rounds return quickly; the hand skin takes a little longer.

## The Nutrition Guardrails

A 20-minute AMRAP costs roughly 200–300 kcal. That is real but not large, and it is not a licence to eat it back twice.

- **Calories at maintenance.** Weekly weigh-ins, 7-day average, hold it within about 2 lb
- **Protein 1.6–2.0 g/kg bodyweight** — this is how the work becomes tissue retained rather than tissue burned
- **Eat something 1–2 hours before.** Fasted 20-minute efforts are fine occasionally and miserable as a habit; performance drops and the log gets noisy
- **Creatine monohydrate, 5 g/day.** Strong evidence for repeated high-effort work of exactly this kind

> [Source: Morton et al. (2018) — protein intake and resistance training outcomes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/) [STRONG]

See [Recovery Nutrition](content/exercise/recovery/recovery-nutrition.md) for timing around sessions.

## When to Back Off

- **Low-back tightness that outlasts the next morning** after swings — a hinge fault, not a strength issue. Drop a bell size, film from the side, and cue *hike it back, stand up hard*. If it refers down a leg, stop hinging and see someone
- **Hand tears** — stop the session. File calluses flat, keep hands dry, use chalk. A torn hand costs a week; a hook-gripped bell costs nothing
- **Shoulder pinch on the press** — switch to push press or floor press for the block, and check that the bell sits on the forearm with the wrist straight in the rack
- **Rounds falling across three sessions** at the same bell with normal sleep and food — you need a deload, not more effort
- **Resting heart rate up 7+ bpm for several days,** or sleep degrading — systemic fatigue. Take the reset week early
