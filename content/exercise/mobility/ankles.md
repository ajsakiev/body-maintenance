# Ankle Mobility

Ankle dorsiflexion — the ability to bring your toes toward your shin — is the most underappreciated mobility limitation in the body. When the ankle can't move, the knee, hip, and lower back compensate. It is a primary driver of knee valgus (inward collapse), heel rise in squats, and altered gait mechanics.

> **Pilates note:** Pilates pays significant attention to pointed-toe ankle work (plantarflexion) but rarely loads dorsiflexion end-range. That's the gap this page addresses.

---

## Self-Assessment

**The Wall Test (most reliable field assessment):**
1. Stand facing a wall, toes 4 inches from the wall
2. Drive your front knee forward toward the wall, keeping your heel flat on the floor
3. Can your knee touch the wall without your heel lifting?

| Distance | Interpretation |
|----------|---------------|
| Knee touches wall at 4+ inches | Good dorsiflexion |
| Knee touches wall only at 3 inches | Mild restriction |
| Heel lifts before wall contact | Significant restriction |

Check both sides — asymmetry is clinically significant. A difference of > 1 inch between sides predicts ankle injury. [MODERATE]

> [Source: Hoch & McKeon (2011) — dorsiflexion asymmetry and ankle injury risk](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21212520/)

---

## Why Ankle Restriction Happens

The most common limiters:
1. **Soft tissue restriction** — calf (gastrocnemius), soleus, and Achilles tightness
2. **Joint capsule restriction** — the talus doesn't slide posteriorly enough in the mortise
3. **Bony restriction** — less common; requires imaging to confirm; not addressable with stretching

The banded mobilization below targets the joint capsule (most under-addressed). The stretches target the soft tissue.

## Exercises

### 1. Banded Dorsiflexion Mobilization

**What it targets:** Ankle joint capsule (talus posterior glide)  
**Time:** 2–3 min/side  
**When:** Before squatting, lunging, or Pilates; before any of the stretches below

**Setup:** You need a resistance band anchored at floor level (loop around a fixed pole or put it under a door).

**How to do it:**
1. Loop the band around the ankle bone (front, where the top of your foot meets the shin)
2. Step far enough away that the band pulls your ankle *forward* with moderate tension
3. Take a lunge position, front foot on the side being worked
4. Drive your front knee forward over your toes — the band creates a posterior force on the talus, encouraging the correct sliding motion
5. 15–20 slow repetitions, going as far forward as possible each time
6. Keep the heel flat throughout

**What to feel:** A deep mobilization in the front of the ankle. Not pain — traction and movement.

> This technique (joint distraction mobilization) addresses the posterior glide restriction of the talus that static calf stretching misses entirely. It is the most effective ankle mobility technique for improving squat mechanics. [MODERATE]

---

### 2. Gastrocnemius Stretch (Straight-Leg Calf)

**What it targets:** Gastrocnemius (the two-headed calf muscle; crosses the knee)  
**Time:** 60–90 sec/side  
**When:** After exercise, evening — not before dynamic activity

**How to do it:**
1. Stand facing a wall, hands on wall
2. Step one foot back 2–3 feet, heel flat on the floor
3. Keep the rear leg completely straight (this is what isolates the gastrocnemius)
4. Lean into the wall until you feel a deep stretch in the upper calf
5. Hold 60–90 sec; breathe into the stretch

**Important:** Hold for at least 60 seconds. Stretches under 30 seconds produce temporary change only. [STRONG]

---

### 3. Soleus Stretch (Bent-Knee Calf)

**What it targets:** Soleus (the deeper calf muscle; does NOT cross the knee; limits dorsiflexion more than gastrocnemius for most people)  
**Time:** 60–90 sec/side  
**When:** Any time; especially if the wall test showed more restriction than expected from calf stretching alone

**How to do it:**
1. Same starting position as the gastrocnemius stretch
2. **Bend the rear knee slightly** — this takes the gastrocnemius off tension and isolates the soleus
3. Drive the bent knee forward over the toes
4. The stretch will feel lower, closer to the heel/Achilles junction

**Why both:** Most people only stretch the gastrocnemius (straight-leg). The soleus is often the greater dorsiflexion limiter because it's harder to stretch passively. [MODERATE]

---

### 4. Ankle CARs (Controlled Articular Rotations)

**What it targets:** Full ankle joint range in all planes; joint health and neurological ownership  
**Time:** 1 min/side  
**When:** Every morning

**How to do it:**
1. Seated or standing on one leg
2. Lift the opposite foot slightly off the floor
3. Make the largest possible circle with the foot — trace a big circle with your toes
4. Keep all motion at the ankle; keep the knee still
5. 5 circles each direction, each foot

**What to feel:** Range of motion that doesn't have joint resistance or grabbing. If you feel clicking or catching, go slower and smaller — don't force through it.

---

### 5. Elevated Heel Squat (Mobility Drill)

**What it targets:** End-range ankle dorsiflexion under load — the most functional strengthening of new range  
**Time:** 2 min  
**When:** After mobilization work; before or after Pilates

**How to do it:**
1. Stand with heels elevated on a 2-inch surface (a weight plate, book, or door threshold)
2. Squat as deep as possible with feet close together
3. At the bottom, push knees apart and hold for 5–10 sec
4. Rise and repeat — 10 reps
5. Over weeks, reduce heel elevation as ankle mobility improves

**Purpose:** Building strength and neurological control at end-range dorsiflexion, so the new range gained in stretching becomes usable. Range without strength is not reliable. [MODERATE]

---

### 6. Toe Walking + Heel Walking

**What it targets:** Strengthening the ankle through its full range — plantarflexion and dorsiflexion  
**Time:** 2 min  
**When:** As a warmup; any time

**How to do it:**
- Walk 15–20 steps on your tiptoes (load plantarflexion)
- Walk 15–20 steps on your heels with toes raised (load dorsiflexion)
- 2 rounds

**Why:** Most ankle mobility work is passive stretching. Loading the end ranges builds the active mobility that actually transfers to movement. [MODERATE]

---

## Weekly Prescription

| Priority | Exercise | Duration | When |
|---------|----------|----------|------|
| Daily | Ankle CARs | 2 min | Morning |
| 3–4×/week | Banded dorsiflexion | 4 min | Pre-squat or pre-Pilates |
| 3–4×/week | Soleus stretch | 3 min | After activity |
| 3×/week | Gastrocnemius stretch | 3 min | After activity |
| 2×/week | Elevated heel squat | 2 min | After mobilization |

> Ankle mobility improvements are typically noticeable within 3–4 weeks of consistent work. The wall test is the best objective marker of progress — test monthly.
