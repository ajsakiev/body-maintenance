# Zone 2 Running Program

A twelve-week running plan whose only job is to build an aerobic base — and, once that base exists, to raise the ceiling on top of it. Three runs a week, all by time rather than distance, almost all of them slow.

[Zone 2 Training](content/exercise/cardio/zone2-training.md) explains what Zone 2 is and how to find your heart-rate range. This page is what to do with that number. It assumes you can walk briskly for 45 minutes and are not currently injured; it does not assume you have run recently, or ever.

---

## Why Running, and the Catch

Running is the most time-efficient Zone 2 modality, it works anywhere with no equipment, it loads bone in a way that cycling and rowing do not, and it is the activity that makes your watch's VO₂ max estimate meaningful. Habitual runners also show substantially lower all-cause and cardiovascular mortality than non-runners, even at modest weekly doses. [STRONG]

> [Source: Lee et al. (2014) — leisure-time running and all-cause mortality, 55,000 adults](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Lee+2014+leisure-time+running+reduces+all-cause+cardiovascular+mortality)

> [Source: Scofield & Hecht (2012) — bone health in endurance athletes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Scofield+Hecht+2012+bone+health+endurance+athletes+running) [MODERATE]

**The catch is twofold**, and the program is built around both:

1. **For most people, running is not a Zone 2 activity yet.** An untrained aerobic system pushes heart rate straight through the top of Zone 2 the moment you break into a jog. The first block of this program is run/walk for exactly that reason — you are teaching your body to run slowly enough to stay in the zone, and that skill takes weeks.
2. **Running injures people through volume, not intensity.** Tendons, bone, and fascia adapt far more slowly than the heart and lungs. Around 6 months in, you will feel able to run much more than your tissues can tolerate. Weekly volume increases of more than about 30% are where the injuries cluster. [MODERATE]

> [Source: Nielsen et al. (2014) — weekly running distance progression and injury risk](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Nielsen+2014+excessive+progression+weekly+running+distance+running-related+injuries)

## The Design in One Table

| Variable | Setting | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 3 runs/week (2 minimum, 4 maximum) | Tissue adapts between sessions, not during them |
| Unit | **Time, not distance** | Distance targets tempt you to run faster; time targets don't care |
| Intensity | 90%+ of all running at or below the top of Zone 2 | The stimulus is sustained, easy work — see the 80/20 evidence below |
| Heart-rate cap | Top of your Zone 2 range (≈70% of max HR) | The governor. When you cross it, you walk |
| Weekly progression | +10% duration or less, and every 4th week cut by ~30% | Keeps you well under the 30% threshold where injuries cluster |
| Intensity work | None until week 9; then one session a week | Base first, ceiling second |
| Cadence | 170–180 steps per minute | Shorter, quicker steps reduce loading per stride |
| Effort test | Full sentences, nose breathing possible | If you can't, you're in Zone 3, and the session is doing a different thing |

The 80/20 split — most volume easy, a small fraction hard, almost nothing in between — is the best-evidenced way to distribute endurance training, and it is what this program produces once intervals arrive in Block 3. [STRONG]

> [Source: Seiler (2010) — best practice for training intensity distribution](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Seiler+2010+training+intensity+distribution+endurance+athletes)

> [Source: Esteve-Lanao et al. (2007) — training intensity distribution and endurance performance](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Esteve-Lanao+2007+impact+training+intensity+distribution+performance+endurance) [MODERATE]

## The Five Session Types

| Session | What it is | HR | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Easy run** | 30–50 min at conversational pace | Zone 2, capped | The bread and butter — 2 of every 3 sessions |
| **Long run** | The week's longest run, extended slowly | Zone 2, capped | Sustained mitochondrial signal; where the base is actually built |
| **Strides** | 4–6 × 20 seconds fast-but-relaxed, full recovery, tacked onto the end of an easy run | HR irrelevant — too short to matter | Running economy and mechanics, without an aerobic cost |
| **Intervals** *(Block 3 only)* | The 4×4 protocol from the [Zone 2 page](content/exercise/cardio/zone2-training.md) | Zone 4–5 | Raises the VO₂ max ceiling the base has been building under |
| **Walk** | 30–45 min brisk | Zone 1–2 | Recovery day that still counts toward the weekly aerobic total |

## The Governor: Run/Walk by Heart Rate

The single most important rule in the program. It is what makes running a Zone 2 activity for someone whose running is not yet Zone 2.

1. Set your watch to show current heart rate, and know your top-of-Zone-2 number
2. Run easy. **When HR crosses the cap, walk.**
3. Walk until HR drops about 10 bpm below the cap, then run again
4. Repeat for the whole session. Do not count how often you walk; it doesn't matter

In week 1, you may walk half the session. By week 6, the walks will be rare. By week 12, most people run continuously at a pace 60–90 seconds per mile faster than their week-1 running pace — **at the same heart rate.** That is the adaptation, and it is the number to track.

**No heart-rate monitor?** Use the talk test as the cap: the moment you can no longer speak a full sentence, walk. Nose breathing is a slightly stricter version of the same rule and works well for people who find the talk test awkward.

**Expect the pace to be embarrassing.** A Zone 2 run for an untrained adult is often 12–14 minutes per mile with walking breaks. This has no bearing on what your pace will be in six months. The people who fail this program are the ones who can't tolerate being slow for four weeks.

## The Twelve-Week Arc

Three blocks and a test week. Sessions are in minutes of total time, including walk breaks. Every fourth week is a step back — that is when the tissues catch up.

| Week | Block | Run 1 | Run 2 | Run 3 *(long)* | Weekly total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Walk/Run | 25 | 25 | 30 | 80 |
| 2 | Walk/Run | 30 | 25 | 35 | 90 |
| 3 | Walk/Run | 30 | 30 | 40 | 100 |
| 4 | *step back* | 25 | 20 | 30 | 75 |
| 5 | Continuous | 35 | 30 | 45 | 110 |
| 6 | Continuous | 35 | 30 + strides | 50 | 115 |
| 7 | Continuous | 40 | 35 + strides | 55 | 130 |
| 8 | *step back* | 30 | 25 | 40 | 95 |
| 9 | Build | 40 + strides | **Intervals** | 60 | 140 |
| 10 | Build | 45 + strides | **Intervals** | 65 | 150 |
| 11 | Build | 45 + strides | **Intervals** | 70 | 155 |
| 12 | *test* | 30 easy | **Benchmark** | 45 | 105 |

### Block 1 — Walk/Run (Weeks 1–4)

The block that decides whether you are a runner in six months or someone who "tried running and their knees didn't like it."

- **Every session is governed by the HR cap.** Walking is not failure; it is the program
- Run on the softest flat surface you have easy access to — trail, track, grass, treadmill. Save pavement for later
- Do not buy new shoes yet unless your current pair is worn out or wrong for you. Change one variable at a time
- Expect calf and shin tightness in weeks 1–2 that fades by week 3. Pain that changes how you walk is a different thing — see *When to Back Off*
- Do the [Daily Mobility Routine](content/exercise/mobility/daily-routine.md) on run days, biased to [ankles](content/exercise/mobility/ankles.md) and [hips](content/exercise/mobility/hips.md)

### Block 2 — Continuous (Weeks 5–8)

- Walk breaks thin out on their own. Do not force it — the cap still governs
- **Strides arrive in week 6.** After an easy run: 4 × 20 seconds at roughly mile-race effort, smooth and relaxed rather than sprinting, with a full 60–90 seconds of walking between. They teach your legs to turn over quickly; they are not a workout
- The long run crosses 50 minutes — the threshold where the sustained-stimulus benefits become clearly worth the extra time
- If a session leaves you needing to lie down, it was not Zone 2. Slow the next one

### Block 3 — Build (Weeks 9–11)

- **The first interval session.** Run 2 becomes the 4×4: 10 min easy, then 4 × (4 min at 90–95% max HR / 3 min easy jog or walk), then 10 min easy. The last minute of each rep should feel like a genuine effort; the first minute should not
- Everything else stays easy — this is what makes the hard day recoverable. If your easy runs drift upward in effort in this block, it will show up as flat interval performance and a rising resting heart rate
- The long run reaches 70 minutes. Bring water on anything over an hour in warm weather

> [Source: Helgerud et al. (2007) — 4×4 interval training and VO₂ max](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17414804/) [STRONG]

### Week 12 — Test

- One short easy run early in the week
- **The Benchmark** (below), rested
- One easy long run to close, then restart at week 5 — the walk/run block does not need repeating unless you've had significant time off

## The Benchmark

The test that tells you the base is growing: **pace at a fixed heart rate.**

**Protocol:** on a flat, repeatable route, warm up 10 minutes, then run **30 minutes holding your heart rate at the top of Zone 2** — not above, not well below. Log the distance covered and the average pace. Same route, same time of day, similar weather, not the day after a hard session.

Run it in week 1 (it will involve walking, which is fine — log the distance anyway), then week 12, then every 8–12 weeks after. Realistic expectations for someone starting from little running: **10–20% more distance in the same 30 minutes at the same heart rate** over the first twelve weeks. That is the aerobic base, measured directly.

**Two supporting numbers:**

- **Resting heart rate**, from your watch, as a weekly average. It should drift down over the program. A sudden rise of 5–7 bpm that persists is a fatigue or illness flag
- **Watch VO₂ max estimate** — see [VO₂ Max & Measurement](content/exercise/cardio/vo2-max.md). The monthly 20-minute moderately-hard outdoor run that keeps it accurate slots naturally into the interval day

## Progression Rules

**1. Time, never distance.** A 40-minute run is 40 minutes whether it covers 3 miles or 5. Distance targets are what turn Zone 2 sessions into Zone 3 sessions.

**2. Ten percent, and a step-back week.** Weekly total up by no more than about 10%, and every fourth week down by 25–30%. The 10% figure is a heuristic rather than a finding — what the evidence actually shows is that jumps beyond ~30% are where injury risk climbs sharply. Staying at 10% keeps you far inside that. [MODERATE]

**3. Extend the long run first.** When adding minutes, add them to the long run before the easy runs. One long stimulus beats three slightly longer short ones.

**4. The cap wins.** If the schedule says 45 minutes and the cap has you walking at minute 30, walk. Time-on-feet still counts. Never trade the cap for the plan.

**5. Change one thing per block.** Shoes, surface, volume, intensity — one at a time. When something hurts, you want to know what caused it.

## Running Form: The Only Three Things Worth Cueing

Running technique is mostly self-organising, and over-coaching it makes people worse. Three cues have evidence behind them and are worth adopting.

| Cue | What it does | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Cadence 170–180** | Shorter, quicker steps reduce braking and joint loading per stride | Count steps for 30 seconds and double it. If you're under 165, raise it 5% at a time using a metronome app or a playlist at ~175 bpm |
| **Land under your hips** | Removes the heel-first overstride that shin splints and knee pain come from | Follows naturally from cadence. Don't think about the foot; think about not reaching forward |
| **Tall and relaxed** | Frees the diaphragm and hip extension | Eyes on the horizon, shoulders down, hands loose enough to hold a chip without breaking it |

> [Source: Heiderscheit et al. (2011) — step rate manipulation and joint mechanics during running](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Heiderscheit+2011+effects+of+step+rate+manipulation+on+joint+mechanics+during+running) [MODERATE]

**On shoes:** a neutral, moderately cushioned trainer that fits is enough. Do not switch to minimalist or maximalist shoes mid-program. Replace them somewhere around 300–500 miles or when the midsole feels dead — for this program, that's roughly once a year.

## Combining With the Kettlebell and Gym Programs

Running, the [Kettlebell AMRAP Program](content/exercise/strength/kettlebell-amrap.md), and the [Year One Gym Program](content/exercise/strength/gym-program.md) all coexist, but not at full dose simultaneously. Two workable weeks:

### Running + kettlebells *(recommended)*

The kettlebell **Pace** day and an easy run do the same aerobic job, so the run replaces it. Hard days are paired so easy days stay easy.

| Day | Session |
|---|---|
| Mon | Kettlebell — Heavy |
| Tue | Easy run |
| Wed | Kettlebell — Push |
| Thu | Easy run *(+ strides / intervals in Block 3)* |
| Fri | Rest or walk |
| Sat | Long run |
| Sun | Rest |

### Running + kettlebells + gym

Only once the running base is built (after week 8) and only with the kettlebell program cut to two sessions. Otherwise the hinge and impact volume on the low back and calves adds up faster than you'd expect.

| Day | Session |
|---|---|
| Mon | Gym A |
| Tue | Easy run |
| Wed | Kettlebell — Push |
| Thu | Easy run + strides |
| Fri | Gym B *(or Kettlebell — Heavy)* |
| Sat | Long run |
| Sun | Rest |

**Two rules for any combination:**

- **Intervals and the kettlebell Push day are both hard days.** Keep at least 48 hours between them
- **Run before you lift if they share a day**, unless the run is the long run — then lift the day before, not after. Easy running after lifting is fine; long running on pre-fatigued legs is where form falls apart

The interference between strength and endurance training is real but small at these volumes, and it mostly affects power, not strength. See the kettlebell page for the evidence. [MODERATE]

## What to Track

```
Date | Type (easy / long / strides / intervals / walk) | Minutes | Avg HR | Pace | Walk breaks? | RPE /10 | Notes
```

Pace at a given HR is the progress metric; RPE and walk breaks are the honesty check. If pace is improving but RPE is climbing too, you're drifting above the cap. Review at each step-back week.

## Missed Time

| Time off | What to do |
|---|---|
| Up to 1 week | Nothing. Resume the week you were in |
| 2–3 weeks | Repeat the last step-back week, then continue |
| 4–8 weeks | Go back two weeks in the plan |
| 2+ months | Restart Block 1. The walk/run block protects the tissues that de-adapt fastest |

Aerobic fitness fades faster than strength — expect the first week back to feel worse than you'd like. Tendon tolerance fades faster still, which is why the return is always via the schedule and never via "I remember being able to run 60 minutes."

## When to Back Off

Running pain has a usable rule: **pain up to about 3/10 that does not worsen during the run and is gone by the next morning is acceptable.** Anything beyond that is a signal to act. [MODERATE]

> [Source: Silbernagel et al. (2007) — pain-monitoring model in tendon loading](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Silbernagel+2007+continued+sports+activity+pain-monitoring+model+Achilles+tendinopathy)

- **Pain that changes your gait** — stop the run, walk home, skip the next session. Limping through a run is how a niggle becomes an injury
- **Shin pain along the inner bone, worse with each run** — reduce volume by a third for two weeks and check cadence. If it localises to a single tender spot on the bone, stop running and get it looked at — that pattern is a stress reaction until proven otherwise
- **Achilles or heel stiffness in the morning** that eases as you walk — early tendon irritation. Drop the strides, add calf raises (slow, 3 × 15, daily), keep easy running if it stays under the pain rule
- **Knee pain that arrives at the same minute every run** — usually a loading issue: cut the long run back, raise cadence, and see [Hips](content/exercise/mobility/hips.md) — most runner's knee traces to the hip
- **Resting heart rate up 5–7 bpm for several days,** sleep degrading, or easy runs feeling hard — take the step-back week early

See [DOMS & Soreness](content/exercise/recovery/doms-soreness.md), [Recovery & Sleep](content/exercise/recovery-sleep.md), and [Soft Tissue Work](content/exercise/recovery/soft-tissue.md).

## Fuelling

Nothing about this program requires eating differently, and most of it should be done on an ordinary diet.

- **Runs under 75 minutes need no fuel during.** Water in warm weather, nothing else
- **Easy runs are fine fasted** if that's convenient, and fine fed. Intervals and the long run are better with something 1–2 hours before
- **Calories at maintenance**, as in the strength programs. Running at these volumes costs 300–600 kcal per session; hunger tends to rise to meet it, which is fine, but "I ran, so I can eat anything" is how running fails to change body composition
- **Protein 1.6–2.0 g/kg** still applies — endurance training on its own erodes muscle if intake is low

See [Recovery Nutrition](content/exercise/recovery/recovery-nutrition.md).
