# Thermal Therapy

Heat and cold both influence recovery — through different mechanisms, at different time points. Used strategically, they're useful adjuncts. Used thoughtlessly, they can blunt the adaptation you trained for.

---

## The Core Trade-Off

Both heat and cold work by modulating inflammation. The key tension: **the inflammatory response after training is part of the adaptation signal.** Aggressively suppressing inflammation immediately post-training may reduce soreness at the cost of reducing adaptation (hypertrophy, strength gains).

**Practical rule:** If your goal is recovery *speed* (athlete competing again soon), suppress freely. If your goal is *long-term adaptation* (building size and strength), be more conservative — especially right after training.

---

## Cold Therapy

### Cold Water Immersion (CWI) [MODERATE]

Immersion in cold water (10–15°C / 50–59°F) for 10–15 minutes after training:

**What the evidence shows:**
- Reduces perceived soreness in the 24–48 hours following training [MODERATE]
- Reduces swelling and tissue temperature
- May speed return to perceived readiness for the next session [MODERATE]
- **Does not speed actual tissue repair** — reduces the perception of soreness

**The adaptation caveat:** [MODERATE]
Habitual cold water immersion immediately post-resistance training appears to **blunt hypertrophy** by suppressing the mTOR signaling pathway and satellite cell activation that drives muscle growth.

> [Source: Roberts et al. (2015) — cold water immersion and hypertrophy, Journal of Physiology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26174323/)

**Best use case:** After endurance training, competition, or when rapid recovery matters more than maximizing adaptation. Less ideal if you're in a strength/hypertrophy phase.

### Cold Showers [EMERGING]

Less evidence than CWI but more accessible. Contrast showers (alternating hot/cold) may offer mild circulatory benefits via vasodilation/vasoconstriction cycling. The effect is significantly weaker than full immersion.

### Cryotherapy (Cryo-chambers) [EMERGING]

Whole-body cryotherapy (−100°C for 2–3 minutes) is promoted aggressively by recovery industry marketing. The evidence is mixed and less robust than cold water immersion. The exposure duration is short; it's unclear whether tissue temperature changes meaningfully.

Not recommended over CWI based on current evidence.

## Heat Therapy

### Sauna [MODERATE — STRONG for cardiovascular; MODERATE for recovery]

Regular sauna use (traditional Finnish sauna at 80–100°C for 15–20 minutes) has meaningful evidence across multiple domains:

**Cardiovascular and longevity effects:** [STRONG]
- 2–3x/week sauna use associated with significantly reduced cardiovascular mortality in the Kuopio cohort study (Finnish men, 20-year follow-up)
- Sauna mimics moderate-intensity cardiovascular exercise: raises heart rate to 120–150 bpm, increases cardiac output
- Appears to maintain or improve arterial compliance

> [Source: Laukkanen et al. (2018) — sauna and cardiovascular health](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29908896/)

**Recovery-specific effects:** [MODERATE]
- Increases blood flow to muscles via vasodilation
- May accelerate removal of metabolic byproducts
- Some evidence for increased growth hormone release (particularly with repeated sessions and heat stress) [EMERGING]
- Reduces muscle stiffness and perceived soreness

**Timing for recovery:**
- Post-training sauna (30+ minutes after) does not appear to blunt hypertrophy the way CWI does [MODERATE]
- Sauna before bed (1–2 hours before) can aid sleep onset via the subsequent core temperature drop

**Protocols:**
| Goal | Protocol |
|---|---|
| Recovery / soreness | 15–20 min at 80–90°C, 2–3 rounds with 5 min cooling breaks |
| Cardiovascular adaptation | Same; aim for 4x/week minimum for longevity effects |
| Post-training recovery | Wait 30+ min after training to avoid excessive dehydration; rehydrate first |

**Contraindications:** Avoid sauna if acutely ill, pregnant, or if you have cardiovascular conditions (consult physician). Always exit if you feel dizzy or nauseous.

### Heat Packs / Local Heat [MODERATE]

Applied heat (heating pad, hot bath) vasodilates local tissue, increases blood flow, and reduces perceived stiffness. Effective for chronic muscle tension and stiffness. Less evidence for DOMS recovery specifically than full-body sauna.

Best used for targeted areas (tight shoulders, lower back) or as pre-activity warm-up for stiff joints.

---

## Contrast Therapy (Alternating Hot/Cold) [MODERATE]

Alternating between hot and cold creates a vascular pumping effect: heat vasodilates, cold vasoconstricts. The repeated cycling increases blood flow turnover in the tissue.

**Protocol:**
- 3–4 rounds
- 3–4 minutes heat (hot tub, sauna) → 1–2 minutes cold (cold plunge, cold shower)
- End on cold

**Evidence:** Moderate positive effects on perceived soreness and performance recovery, particularly in team sport athletes. Mechanistically plausible. Not as well-studied as CWI or sauna alone.

---

## Putting It Together

| Goal | Tool | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| Fast soreness relief | Cold water immersion (10–15°C, 10–15 min) | Within 1–2 hrs post-training |
| Long-term adaptation (hypertrophy) | Sauna or contrast (avoid CWI immediately post-training) | 30+ min post-training or on recovery days |
| Stiffness / morning soreness | Local heat (pad or hot shower) | Before movement |
| Cardiovascular health + recovery | Regular sauna (4x/week) | Flexible; integrate into routine |
| Whole-body recovery day | Contrast shower or sauna + light movement | Any time during recovery day |
