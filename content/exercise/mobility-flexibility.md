# Mobility & Flexibility

Mobility is the ability to actively move a joint through its full range of motion under control. Flexibility is the passive range available. Both decline sharply with age and inactivity — but unlike many aspects of aging, they respond well to targeted training at any age.

---

## Why It Matters

Loss of mobility is not a passive consequence of aging — it is a consequence of movement deprivation. [STRONG]

Reduced hip mobility predicts fall risk in older adults. [STRONG] Thoracic stiffness contributes to shoulder impingement and neck pain. [MODERATE] Limited ankle dorsiflexion alters gait mechanics and increases knee injury risk. [MODERATE]

Beyond injury prevention, mobility training:
- Reduces chronic musculoskeletal pain [STRONG]
- Improves athletic performance and economy of movement [MODERATE]
- Appears to reduce all-cause mortality in studies measuring sit-and-rise score [MODERATE]

> **Key study:** Araújo et al. (2012) found that adults who scored poorly on the sitting-rising test (requiring hip mobility, flexibility, and balance) had 5–6x higher mortality risk over 6 years. [Source](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22948714/)

## Core Principles

### 1. Distinguish Mobility from Flexibility

**Flexibility** = passive range (how far someone can move you)
**Mobility** = active range (how far you can move yourself, under control)

Passive flexibility without active control is not functional — and can increase injury risk. The goal is to build *usable* range of motion you can load and control, not just achieve extreme passive stretches.

### 2. The Joint-by-Joint Model [MODERATE]

Each joint has a primary role: either **stability** (resisting movement) or **mobility** (producing movement). Problems at one joint often manifest as pain at the adjacent joint.

| Joint | Primary Need |
|-------|-------------|
| Foot/ankle | Mobility (dorsiflexion) |
| Knee | Stability |
| Hip | Mobility (all planes) |
| Lumbar spine | Stability |
| Thoracic spine | Mobility (rotation, extension) |
| Shoulder (glenohumeral) | Mobility |
| Scapulothoracic | Stability |
| Cervical spine | Mobility |

Most people are stiff in ankles, hips, and thoracic spine — and use their lumbar spine (which should be stable) to compensate. This is a primary driver of lower back pain. [STRONG]

### 3. Static Stretching: Limited Long-Term Benefit for Mobility

Static stretching (holding a stretch for 30–60s) improves passive flexibility but has limited transfer to functional mobility. [STRONG]

Static stretching immediately before strength or power activity **reduces performance** by 4–8%. [STRONG] It is best used for general tissue relaxation post-workout or before sleep — not as a mobility training method.

> [Source: Behm & Chaouachi, 2011 — meta-analysis of acute stretching effects](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21373870/)

### 4. Dynamic Mobility Work Transfers Better

Dynamic movements — leg swings, hip circles, shoulder rotations performed under active control — prepare joints for load and improve functional range over time. [MODERATE]

### 5. PAILs & RAILs: The Most Effective Mobility Method [MODERATE]

**Progressive Angular Isometric Loading (PAILs) and Regressive Angular Isometric Loading (RAILs)** were developed by Dr. Andreo Spina as part of Functional Range Conditioning (FRC). This approach uses isometric contractions at end range to:

1. Neurologically "accept" the new range as safe
2. Build strength in the newly acquired range
3. Drive lasting tissue adaptation

This is more effective than passive stretching because it creates **both** new range *and* the muscular control to use it. [MODERATE]

## Key Joint Protocols

### Hips — The Most Important Joint

The hip is the largest joint in the body and the center of athletic movement. Most adults have significant restriction in:
- **Hip flexion** (sitting shortens hip flexors)
- **Hip internal rotation** (most undertrained direction)
- **Hip extension** (gluteal inhibition from prolonged sitting)

**90/90 Hip Stretch with PAILs/RAILs**

1. Sit in 90/90 position (front shin parallel to your body, rear shin parallel behind)
2. Passively sink into the stretch for 2 minutes to allow tissue to relax
3. **PAILs:** Press your front knee gently into the floor (6–10 second isometric, 50–70% effort)
4. **RAILs:** Actively pull your front leg *up* against the floor (6–10 second isometric)
5. Relax, then try to push further into the stretch with your own muscles
6. 2–3 sets per side

> Minimum dose: 5 minutes/side of hip mobility work daily produces measurable improvement within 4–6 weeks. [MODERATE]

### Thoracic Spine — The Most Neglected Area

Most desk workers lose thoracic rotation and extension. This forces the lumbar spine and shoulders to compensate, driving the two most common pain patterns.

**Thoracic Extension Over Foam Roller**
1. Place foam roller across mid-back (T4–T8 region)
2. Support your head with hands behind neck
3. Extend over the roller, breathing out to relax
4. Move the roller up and down through T4–T10
5. 60–90 seconds total

**Thoracic Rotation (Quadruped)**
1. Quadruped position (hands and knees)
2. Place one hand behind head
3. Rotate that elbow toward the opposite elbow, then open fully to the ceiling
4. 10–12 reps per side, focusing on ribcage rotation, not lumbar

### Shoulders — Overhead Mobility

Most people lack full shoulder flexion and external rotation. This limits overhead pressing and pulling and contributes to rotator cuff stress.

**Banded Shoulder Distraction**
1. Anchor a resistance band at shoulder height
2. Loop band around upper arm (near armpit)
3. Step away to create tension, arm straight
4. Slowly explore flexion and external rotation while the band distracts the joint
5. 60–90 seconds per side

**Doorway Pec Stretch + Active Pull**
1. Place forearm on doorframe at 90°
2. Step through until you feel a chest stretch
3. Hold 30s passive
4. Then actively pull your arm *forward* (into the doorframe) for 10 seconds — PAILs
5. Release and try to pull further back into stretch

### Ankles — The Forgotten Joint

Limited dorsiflexion (ankle bends when squatting) forces the knee to track inward or the heel to rise — major contributors to knee pain and poor squat mechanics.

**Ankle Dorsiflexion Mobilization (Banded)**
1. Anchor a resistance band low, loop around ankle at the front
2. Step away to create tension (band pulling ankle forward)
3. Drive knee forward over toes in a slow, controlled lunge
4. 15 reps per side, 2–3 sets
5. Test: can you touch your knee to a wall with toes 4–5 inches from the wall?

## Daily Movement Minimum

The most important habit is **breaking up static positions**. Sitting or standing in one position for hours creates tissue compression and joint restriction faster than any weekly mobility session can reverse.

**The 30-minute rule:** Change position or take a brief movement break every 30 minutes. [MODERATE]

> [Source: Dunstan et al. (2012) — prolonged sitting and metabolic health](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22473953/)

**Minimum effective dose for mobility improvement:**

| Area | Time | Frequency |
|------|------|-----------|
| Hips | 5–10 min | Daily |
| Thoracic spine | 3–5 min | 5x/week |
| Shoulders | 3–5 min | 3–4x/week |
| Ankles | 3–5 min | 3–4x/week |

A 15–20 minute daily mobility routine covering the major areas above produces measurable improvement within 6–8 weeks. [MODERATE]

## Fascia & Connective Tissue

**Fascia** is the connective tissue web surrounding every muscle, organ, and joint. Chronic tension, injury, and inactivity cause fascial adhesions that restrict movement.

**Foam rolling and myofascial release** reduce perceived soreness and temporarily improve range of motion. [MODERATE] Their long-term benefit as a standalone practice is modest — they are best used as a *warm-up* before mobility work, not as a substitute for it.

**Collagen and connective tissue adaptation:**
- Tendons and fascia have poor blood supply and adapt slowly (months, not weeks)
- Loading connective tissue at end range (PAILs/RAILs) drives adaptation
- Vitamin C (500mg) taken 30–60 minutes before connective tissue training may enhance collagen synthesis [EMERGING]

> [Source: Shaw et al. (2017) — vitamin C and tendon collagen synthesis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28177710/)

## Sample Routine

**Morning (10–15 min):**
- 90/90 hip stretch with PAILs/RAILs — 3 min/side
- Thoracic foam rolling + rotation — 3 min
- Banded ankle mobilization — 2 min/side

**Post-workout or evening (10 min):**
- Doorway pec stretch — 2 min/side
- Seated forward fold (passive) — 3 min
- Deep squat hold with prying — 2 min

> **Rule of thumb:** If a joint feels painful or restricted, that's where the work needs to go — not where it's comfortable. Comfort zones in mobility work are where stiffness hides.
