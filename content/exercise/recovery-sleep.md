# Recovery & Sleep

Training is a stimulus. Adaptation happens during recovery. No recovery = no adaptation. Sleep is the single most powerful recovery intervention available — and the most systematically neglected.

---

## Sleep: The Foundation

Sleep is not optional. Chronic short sleep (< 7 hours/night) produces measurable impairments in nearly every domain relevant to health and performance. [STRONG]

### What Sleep Deprivation Does

**Physical:**
- Reduces muscle protein synthesis by ~30% [STRONG]
- Blunts testosterone and growth hormone release [STRONG]
- Increases cortisol and inflammatory markers [STRONG]
- Impairs glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity within days [STRONG]

**Cognitive:**
- Performance on alertness, reaction time, and decision-making tasks declines progressively with each night under 7 hours [STRONG]
- Crucially, **sleep-deprived people are poor judges of their own impairment** — subjective perception adapts; objective performance does not [STRONG]

**Long-term health:**
- Short sleep strongly associated with cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, obesity, and depression [STRONG]
- Chronic short sleep (≤6 hours) associated with 12–13% increased all-cause mortality [STRONG]

> [Source: Cappuccio et al. (2010) — sleep duration and mortality meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20469800/)

> **Matthew Walker, PhD:** "There is no aspect of your health that isn't impaired by sleep deprivation. None." (*Why We Sleep*, 2017)

### Sleep Architecture

A full night of sleep consists of 4–6 cycles (~90 minutes each), each containing:

| Stage | Type | Primary Function |
|-------|------|-----------------|
| N1 | Light NREM | Transition |
| N2 | Deeper NREM | Motor learning, memory consolidation |
| N3 | Deep / Slow-wave | Physical recovery, HGH release, immune function |
| REM | Rapid Eye Movement | Emotional regulation, procedural memory, creativity |

**Deep sleep (N3) dominates the first half of the night. REM sleep dominates the second half.** Cutting sleep short from either end disproportionately loses one type:
- Going to bed late → lose deep sleep
- Waking early → lose REM

Both matter for different aspects of recovery.

## Sleep Targets

| Group | Nightly Target |
|-------|--------------|
| Adults (18–64) | 7–9 hours |
| Adults 65+ | 7–8 hours |
| Athletes | 8–10 hours (high training load) |

**Consistency of schedule matters as much as total duration.** [STRONG] Social jetlag — shifting sleep timing significantly on weekends — impairs metabolic function even when total hours are maintained.

> [Source: Wittmann et al. (2006) — social jetlag and metabolic risk](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16687233/)

## Sleep Hygiene: Evidence-Based Protocols

### Light Management [STRONG]

**Morning light exposure (10–30 minutes outdoors within 30–60 minutes of waking)** is one of the most powerful anchors for the circadian rhythm. Light hits the retina, signals the suprachiasmatic nucleus, and sets the timing of cortisol release, alertness, and melatonin onset ~14–16 hours later. [STRONG]

> [Source: Lewy et al. (1980) — light and circadian rhythm research (foundational)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7348542/)

**Evening light:** Blue and bright white light after sunset suppresses melatonin and delays sleep onset. [STRONG]
- Dim lights 1–2 hours before bed
- Use warm-spectrum lighting (2700K or lower)
- Blue-light blocking glasses or Night Mode on screens provide partial mitigation but are less effective than simply reducing light intensity [MODERATE]

### Temperature [STRONG]

Core body temperature must drop ~1–2°C to initiate and maintain sleep. **A cooler sleeping environment (16–19°C / 60–67°F) significantly improves sleep quality and deep sleep duration.** [STRONG]

> [Source: Czeisler & Gooley (2007) — temperature and sleep](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17413019/)

A warm shower or bath 1–2 hours before bed paradoxically helps: it draws blood to the skin surface, allowing core temperature to drop faster once you're in bed. [MODERATE]

### Caffeine Timing [STRONG]

Caffeine has a half-life of 5–7 hours (varies by genetics). A coffee at 2pm means ~50% of the caffeine is still in your system at 9pm.

**Practical guideline:** No caffeine after 12–1pm if targeting a 10–11pm bedtime. [STRONG]

> [Source: Drake et al. (2013) — caffeine timing and sleep disruption](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24235903/)

**Adenosine rebound:** Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors (the sleep pressure chemical). It doesn't eliminate adenosine — it masks it. The adenosine accumulates; when caffeine clears, the "crash" hits. This is why late-afternoon caffeine disrupts sleep even when people feel fine.

### Alcohol [STRONG]

Alcohol is widely perceived as a sleep aid. It is not. [STRONG]

Alcohol:
- **Suppresses REM sleep**, fragmenting sleep architecture in the second half of the night
- Increases overnight heart rate and body temperature
- Acts as a diuretic, increasing nocturnal waking
- Reduces sleep quality even at doses as low as 1–2 drinks for women and 2–3 drinks for men

There is no "safe" amount of alcohol that does not impair sleep quality. [STRONG]

> [Source: Ebrahim et al. (2013) — alcohol and sleep meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24235903/)

### Consistency Over Everything [STRONG]

**A fixed wake time is more important than a fixed bedtime.** The sleep drive (adenosine accumulation) governs how sleepy you are. A consistent wake time anchors your circadian clock, which in turn stabilizes sleep onset.

- Set a fixed wake time and protect it, even on weekends
- If you can't fall asleep, don't lie in bed awake — exit the bedroom and return only when sleepy (sleep restriction therapy) [STRONG]

## Active Recovery

**Active recovery** means low-intensity movement on rest days — enough to increase blood flow and reduce soreness without adding meaningful training stress.

Benefits: [MODERATE]
- Reduces delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS)
- Maintains movement patterns
- Supports lymphatic drainage

**Effective active recovery methods:**
- 20–30 min easy walk or cycle (Zone 1)
- Yoga or light mobility work
- Swimming (easy pace)
- Light rowing

**What doesn't count as active recovery:** Heavy Zone 3 cardio, strength work, or intense sports. These add stress rather than clearing it.

## Heart Rate Variability (HRV)

HRV measures the variation in time between consecutive heartbeats. A healthy heart does not beat like a metronome — small beat-to-beat variations reflect autonomic nervous system responsiveness.

**Higher HRV = better parasympathetic tone = greater recovery and adaptive capacity.** [MODERATE]

HRV is influenced by:
- Sleep quality and quantity (↑ sleep = ↑ HRV)
- Training load (excessive load → acute HRV drop)
- Alcohol (strongly suppresses HRV even in small amounts) [STRONG]
- Illness and stress (↓ HRV is an early warning signal)

**Practical use:**
- Track HRV first thing in the morning, reclining, before getting out of bed
- Use a wearable (Whoop, Garmin, Polar, Oura) or a chest strap with an HRV app
- Look at the **trend** over weeks, not day-to-day fluctuations
- If HRV is trending down over multiple days, it's a signal to reduce training intensity

**HRV should rise gradually over months** as aerobic fitness and recovery quality improve.

## Napping

**20–30 minute naps improve alertness, mood, and performance without impairing nighttime sleep.** [STRONG]

> [Source: Milner & Cote (2009) — napping and performance](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19645971/)

**Rules for productive napping:**
- Keep it under 30 minutes (prevents entering deep sleep and waking groggy)
- Avoid naps after 3pm (can delay sleep onset)
- "Coffee nap": drink coffee immediately before a 20-minute nap — the caffeine kicks in just as you wake up [MODERATE]

## Stress and Recovery

Psychological stress activates the same physiological pathways as physical stress. The body cannot distinguish between training overload and work/life overload — cortisol is cortisol.

**Chronic elevated cortisol:**
- Suppresses testosterone and growth hormone [STRONG]
- Impairs sleep quality [STRONG]
- Increases abdominal fat deposition [STRONG]
- Blunts immune function [STRONG]

Stress management is not soft — it is a physiological necessity for adaptation.

**Evidence-backed stress interventions:**
- Daily moderate-intensity exercise itself [STRONG]
- 10–20 minutes of meditation or structured breathwork [MODERATE]
- Social connection [MODERATE]
- Time in nature [MODERATE]
- Physiological sigh (double inhale through nose, long exhale through mouth) produces the fastest acute reduction in physiological arousal [EMERGING]

> [Source: Balban et al. (2023) — physiological sigh and stress](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36754061/)
