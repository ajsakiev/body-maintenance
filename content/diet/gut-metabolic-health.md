# Gut & Metabolic Health

The gut microbiome — the ~38 trillion bacteria, archaea, and fungi residing primarily in the large intestine — plays a role in metabolism, immunity, inflammation, mental health, and even body composition. Metabolic health (insulin sensitivity, blood glucose regulation, lipid metabolism) is the other half of this picture: the two are deeply intertwined.

---

## The Gut Microbiome: What It Does

The microbiome's influence extends far beyond digestion:

| Function | Mechanism | Evidence |
|----------|-----------|----------|
| Immunity regulation | 70% of immune tissue is gut-associated (GALT) | [STRONG] |
| Inflammation modulation | Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) regulate inflammatory tone | [STRONG] |
| Mental health | Gut-brain axis via vagus nerve and neurotransmitter production | [MODERATE] |
| Metabolic regulation | Influences glucose metabolism, fat storage, and appetite hormones | [MODERATE] |
| Body weight | Microbiome composition differences observed in lean vs. obese individuals | [MODERATE] |

> "The gut microbiome may be the most important modifiable determinant of long-term health that we have direct dietary control over."
> — Sonnenburg & Sonnenburg (Stanford), *The Good Gut* (2015)

**Key principle: diversity is health.** Higher species diversity in the gut microbiome is consistently associated with better health outcomes. Ultra-processed diets, low fiber, antibiotics, and sedentary behavior all reduce diversity. [STRONG]

## Fiber: The Most Underconsumed Nutrient

Most adults in Western countries eat 10–15g of fiber per day. The evidence-based target is 25–38g. This gap may be the single largest dietary deficiency in terms of downstream health consequences. [STRONG]

### Types of Fiber

| Type | What It Does | Sources |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Soluble fiber** | Forms a gel; feeds beneficial bacteria; slows glucose absorption | Oats, beans, apples, flaxseed |
| **Insoluble fiber** | Adds bulk; speeds transit; reduces constipation | Wheat bran, vegetables, whole grains |
| **Resistant starch** | Bypasses small intestine; feeds colon bacteria; produces butyrate | Cooked and cooled potatoes/rice, green bananas, legumes |
| **Prebiotic fiber (FOS, inulin)** | Selectively feeds beneficial bacteria | Garlic, onion, leeks, asparagus, chicory |

**Fiber targets:**

| Group | Daily Target |
|-------|-------------|
| Adult men | 35–38g |
| Adult women | 25–28g |
| General (minimum) | 25g |

> [Source: WHO Dietary Fiber Recommendations; Reynolds et al. (2019) — carbohydrate quality meta-analysis in *The Lancet*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30638909/)

### Fiber and Butyrate

Fermentable fiber (especially resistant starch and FOS) is metabolized by colonic bacteria into **short-chain fatty acids**, primarily **butyrate**. Butyrate is the primary fuel for colonocytes (colon cells), strengthens the gut barrier, and has anti-inflammatory and anti-cancer properties. [STRONG]

Inadequate fiber → inadequate butyrate → gut barrier dysfunction → systemic inflammation ("leaky gut"). [MODERATE]

## Fermented Foods: Rapid Microbiome Impact

In a landmark 2021 randomized controlled trial from the Sonnenburg Lab at Stanford:

> Adults eating a **high-fermented food diet** (yogurt, kefir, fermented cottage cheese, kimchi, kombucha) for 10 weeks showed **increased microbiome diversity** and **reduced 19 inflammatory markers** — compared to a high-fiber diet group.

> [Source: Wastyk et al. (2021) — fermented foods and microbiome, *Cell*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34256014/)

**Best fermented food sources:**

| Food | Live Cultures | Notes |
|------|-------------|-------|
| Plain yogurt | Yes | Choose unsweetened; Greek for higher protein |
| Kefir | Yes | More diverse bacteria than yogurt |
| Kimchi | Yes | Also high in vitamin C and fiber |
| Sauerkraut | Yes | Buy refrigerated (shelf-stable is pasteurized) |
| Miso | Yes | Unpasteurized; use in soups (don't boil) |
| Tempeh | Yes | Fermented soy; excellent protein + probiotics |
| Kombucha | Yes | Moderate amounts; watch added sugar |

**Dose that drove changes in the Stanford study:** 6 servings/day of fermented food. For practical purposes, 2–3 servings/day is a reasonable target. [MODERATE]

## Probiotics: Supplements vs. Food

**Food-based probiotics are superior to most probiotic supplements** for most people. [MODERATE]

Probiotic supplements:
- Most do not colonize the gut long-term (they pass through)
- Strain specificity matters — most products don't contain strains with meaningful evidence
- Expensive

**When supplements may help:** [MODERATE]
- During or after antibiotic use — *Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG* and *Saccharomyces boulardii* have good evidence for antibiotic-associated diarrhea
- Specific gut conditions (IBS, IBD) — evidence for specific strains

**Prebiotics (fiber)** support your *existing* beneficial bacteria. Combined prebiotic + probiotic ("synbiotic") approaches are more promising than probiotic supplementation alone. [MODERATE]

## Blood Sugar & Metabolic Health

**Insulin resistance** — the inability of cells to respond appropriately to insulin — is the root cause of type 2 diabetes, PCOS, and fatty liver disease, and a major driver of cardiovascular disease and cognitive decline.

> ~40% of U.S. adults have metabolic syndrome (a cluster of insulin-resistant conditions). A substantial fraction are undiagnosed. [STRONG]

### Key Metabolic Markers

| Marker | Optimal Range | Concern Threshold |
|--------|-------------|------------------|
| Fasting glucose | 70–90 mg/dL | >100 mg/dL (pre-diabetic) |
| HbA1c | <5.3% | ≥5.7% (pre-diabetic) |
| Fasting insulin | <5–7 μIU/mL | >10 μIU/mL |
| Triglycerides | <100 mg/dL | >150 mg/dL |
| HDL cholesterol | >60 mg/dL | <40 mg/dL (men), <50 mg/dL (women) |

**HOMA-IR** (fasting insulin × fasting glucose / 405) is a calculated insulin resistance index. HOMA-IR >2.5 suggests insulin resistance.

### Blood Sugar Management Through Diet

**Glycemic load (GL), not just glycemic index (GI), is the practical metric.** GL accounts for portion size — a food can have a high GI but low GL (watermelon, for example).

**Strategies to reduce postprandial blood glucose spikes:** [STRONG]

1. **Eat vegetables and protein first, carbohydrates last** — the sequence of food consumption significantly affects glucose response. Eating protein and fiber before starch can reduce the glucose spike by 30–40%.
   > [Source: Shukla et al. (2017) — food order and glucose](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28701389/)

2. **Add vinegar (1–2 tbsp) before or with carbohydrate-heavy meals** — acetic acid blunts glucose absorption. Apple cider vinegar in water before meals has modest but real glucose-lowering effects. [MODERATE]
   > [Source: Johnston et al. (2004) — vinegar and glucose](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15461877/)

3. **Walk 10–20 minutes after eating** — skeletal muscle contraction clears blood glucose without insulin. Even a gentle post-meal walk significantly blunts the glucose spike. [STRONG]
   > [Source: Reynolds et al. (2017) — post-meal walking and glucose](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28630617/)

4. **Choose lower GL carbohydrates** — legumes, oats, sweet potatoes over white bread and sugar-sweetened products

5. **Fiber with every meal** — viscous soluble fiber forms a physical barrier in the gut that slows carbohydrate absorption [STRONG]

## Time-Restricted Eating

**Time-restricted eating (TRE)** — limiting food intake to a defined daily window (typically 8–12 hours) — aligns eating with circadian biology. Most metabolic processes are tuned for daytime activity; eating late at night or over a 16+ hour window disrupts these rhythms. [MODERATE]

**What the evidence shows:**
- 8–10 hour eating window improves fasting glucose and insulin sensitivity in metabolically unhealthy adults [MODERATE]
- Benefits beyond simple caloric reduction (you tend to eat less in a shorter window) are still debated [MODERATE]
- Early TRE (eating earlier in the day) appears superior to late TRE for metabolic outcomes, consistent with circadian biology [MODERATE]

> [Source: Sutton et al. (2018) — early TRE and metabolic health, RCT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29754952/)

**Practical approach:** A 10-hour window (e.g., 8am–6pm or 9am–7pm) captures most of the benefit with minimal lifestyle disruption. Late-night eating (after 9pm) disproportionately impairs metabolic health. [MODERATE]

## Gut Barrier & "Leaky Gut"

**Intestinal permeability** refers to the passage of bacterial products (lipopolysaccharide, LPS) through the gut wall into systemic circulation. This drives systemic inflammation and is associated with metabolic disease and autoimmune conditions. [MODERATE]

> This is commonly called "leaky gut" in popular media. The science is real; many popular supplements marketed around it are not. [MODERATE]

**Evidence-supported ways to maintain gut barrier integrity:**
- Adequate fiber and fermented foods [MODERATE]
- Avoiding chronic NSAID overuse (ibuprofen damages gut lining) [STRONG]
- Adequate sleep [STRONG]
- Managing chronic stress [MODERATE]
- Zinc and glutamine support gut lining integrity [EMERGING]

**What doesn't reliably fix leaky gut:** Most branded "gut healing" supplements with collagen, aloe vera, or L-glutamine in isolation lack RCT evidence. Address the root causes first.

## Practical Summary

**Daily habits:**
1. 25–38g fiber — prioritize diversity of plant sources
2. 2–3 servings fermented food
3. Walk 10–20 minutes after your largest meal
4. Stop eating 2–3 hours before bed
5. Drink mostly water; minimize sugary beverages

**Weekly targets:**
- 30+ different plant foods per week (research suggests this number is a strong predictor of microbiome diversity)
- Legumes 3–4×
- Cruciferous vegetables 4–5×

> [Source: McDonald et al. (2018) — American Gut Project, plant diversity and microbiome](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29795809/)
